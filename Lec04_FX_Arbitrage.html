Section 1 · FX Market Drivers

What Moves Exchange Rates?

Six forces drive currency supply and demand — and they often interact.

📈 Interest Rate Differentials
↑ Higher-rate currency APPRECIATES

Higher rates → stronger currency. Capital flows to higher yields. A surprise Fed hike lifts USD as global investors shift to dollar assets.

📦 Trade Flows
↑ Exporter's domestic currency APPRECIATES

Export surplus → domestic currency demand. Net inflows of foreign currency bid up the exporter's currency. China's persistent current account surplus has supported CNY.

💹 Carry Trade
↑ High-yield target · ↓ Low-yield funding currency

Borrow cheap, invest high. Sell JPY/CHF, buy MXN/AUD. When risk collapses, carry unwinds violently — JPY surges as shorts cover.

🛡️ Safe-Haven Demand
↑ Safe havens (USD / JPY / CHF) APPRECIATE

Crisis → flight to quality. USD, JPY, CHF attract capital in stress. The dollar paradoxically strengthened in 2008 even as the US caused the crisis.

🏛️ Fiscal & Political Risk
↓ Local currency DEPRECIATES

High deficits, instability → currency weakness. Argentina, Turkey, and Brazil illustrate this repeatedly. Sovereign risk premium shows up in FX first.

🏦 Central Bank Intervention
↑↓ Direction set by CB intent

Direct FX purchases / sales. SNB's 1.20 CHF/EUR floor, BOJ yen interventions (2022), China's daily fix. Credibility determines effectiveness.

Section 1 · Cross Rate Analysis

Mispricing Factor

Divide the Implied Rate (what the math says it should be) by the Market Quote (what the broker charges). A ratio above 1 means the market is mispriced — arbitrage profit exists.

📐 Decision Rule
> 1 Market rate is too cheap → arbitrage opportunity ⚡
= 1 Market is fairly priced → no arbitrage
< 1 Market rate is too expensive → reverse the trade
🔢 S3 Example 1 Applied
Implied EUR/GBP: 1.1200 ÷ 1.2700 = 0.8819
Market Quote: 0.8740
0.8819 ÷ 0.8740 = 1.0090
Ratio > 1 → arbitrage exists · +0.90%

Section 3 · FX Arbitrage

The Idea Behind Triangular Arbitrage

Exchange rates form a triangle.
All three sides must be consistent.

If you convert USD → EUR → GBP → USD and end up with more than you started, the rates are inconsistent — and that gap is risk-free profit.

f₁ × f₂ × f₃ = 1.0000

When this product ≠ 1.0000, one of the three quoted rates is out of line. You exploit it by going around the triangle in the profitable direction.

❓ Why multiply? The next slide divides…

Each fᵢ is the effective multiplier for that leg — NOT always the raw quote. When you divide by a rate in Step-by-Step math, that is mathematically identical to multiplying by 1/rate.

Leg (path)Raw QuoteOperationEffective fᵢ
USD → EUREUR/USD = 1.12÷ 1.12f₁ = 1/1.12
EUR → GBPEUR/GBP = 0.874÷ 0.874f₂ = 1/0.874
GBP → USDGBP/USD = 1.27× 1.27f₃ = 1.27

The formula f₁ × f₂ × f₃ = 1 is just saying: multiply all the effective multipliers together — divisions already baked in. No-arb ↔ you end up where you started.

No forecasting needed. No credit risk. You start and end in the same currency — the profit is locked in at execution.
Section 3 · FX Arbitrage

Worked Example — Step by Step

Rates: EUR/USD = 1.1200  ·  GBP/USD = 1.2700  ·  EUR/GBPmkt = 0.8740  ·  Notional: $1,000,000  ·  Path: USD → GBP → EUR → USD

Step 1 — USD → GBP  sell USD, buy cheap GBP
Step 2 — GBP → EUR  market gives MORE EUR than fair value
Step 3 — EUR → USD  convert back at EUR/USD
Why the Opportunity Exists

The three rates are inconsistent: their product is 1.0090 — not 1.0000. That 0.90% gap is the arbitrage profit.

Net Profit
+$9,028
On $1M · zero market risk · seconds to execute
Self-correcting: The trade itself closes the gap — buying EUR bids up EUR/USD, selling EUR/GBP pushes it toward 0.8819. Arb opportunities vanish within milliseconds in electronic markets.
