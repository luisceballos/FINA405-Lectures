Bond types

Fixed or floating: what sets the coupon?

Fixed-rate bond

  • Coupon rate is set in the contract.
  • Payments stay the same as market rates move.
  • Example: $1,000 × 5% ÷ 2 = $25.

Floating rate note (FRN)

  • Coupon resets at the agreed dates.
  • Benchmark rate SOFR + contractual spread.
  • Example: SOFR + 3%; the benchmark changes, the spread stays fixed.

Both have a face value, payment schedule and maturity. The benchmark is not the issuer’s credit spread.

Real corporate FRN: JPMorgan Chase & Co., $400 million notes due December 10, 2025: Compounded SOFR + 0.600%, paid quarterly. Official prospectus · page 1. Historical issuance; its conventions differ from our simplified semiannual example.

The CFO’s problem

Today’s rate is 5%. What if it falls?

  • Our CFO has $1,000 of fixed-rate debt at 5%: $25 every six months.
  • The current SOFR-based rate is also 5%; the CFO expects it to fall to 4%, then 3%.
  • The debt payment will not fall automatically. Could a floating-rate exposure let the company benefit?

Hypothetical teaching scenario, not today’s actual SOFR quote. Expectations are uncertain.

FRN basics

The coupon resets. The principal does not.

  • Floating rate note: a bond with a coupon that changes.
  • Coupon rate = SOFR + contractual spread.
  • Example: six-month SOFR-based rate SOFR + 3%; face value $1,000.

Annual rates; semiannual coupons. The spread stays fixed in this example. The principal is repaid at maturity.

Reset timing

Today’s new rate is for the next payment.

Bond data: FV = $1,000 · annual coupon = SOFR + 3% spread · maturity t=2 · semiannual payments at t=0.5, 1, 1.5, 2. Principal is repaid at t=2.

  • At t=0, SOFR = 3% fixes the first coupon.
  • At t=0.5, pay that coupon; the new 4% rate applies to years 0.5–1.

Illustrative six-month SOFR-based rate fixed in advance, following Lec13. No overnight compounding or day-count adjustment.

The IRS market

A large market for changing rate exposure

  • Banks, companies and investors use swaps to exchange fixed and floating interest exposure.
  • OIS plus other IRS averaged about $7.0 trillion in daily turnover in April 2025.
  • For scale: U.S. nominal GDP was approximately $30.9 trillion for all of 2025 (BEA via FRED). GDP is annual production; the swap figure is daily trading turnover, so these are different measures.

Turnover measures trading activity, not outstanding notional, money lent or losses at risk. OIS = overnight index swaps. Rounded values.

Source: BIS Triennial Survey, April 2025, turnover by instrument.

From FRNs to swaps

Exchange fixed and floating interest.

  • An IRS exchanges interest payments on an agreed notional.
  • Firm A pays floating and receives 5% fixed.
  • Two years; $1,000 notional; six-month payments.

The swap is separate from any borrowing. It does not fund a loan. No principal is exchanged in this single-currency swap.

Firm A’s perspective

Keep payments, receipts and cost separate.

Positive cost: A pays net. Negative cost: A receives net.

Lec13 reports net cash flow as receipt minus payment. Net cost uses the opposite sign. We calculate cash flows, not the swap’s market value.

Worked example

What happens when SOFR = 4%?

  • Rate fixed at the semester’s start: 4%.
  • A pays floating and receives 5% on $1,000.
  1. Floating payment: $1,000 × 4% ÷ 2 = $20.
  2. Fixed receipt: $1,000 × 5% ÷ 2 = $25.
  3. Net cost: $20 − $25 = −$5.
A receives $5 net. B pays $5 net. There is no principal payment under the swap.

Takeaways

Reset first. Pay later. Keep the signs clear.

  • FRN: the coupon resets with the benchmark plus spread.
  • IRS: exchange fixed and floating interest; no funding or principal exchange.
  • Timing: use the start-of-period rate, not the new rate observed on payment day.
