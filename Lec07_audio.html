Section 1 — Basics
What is a Bond?
A bond is a loan — the issuer borrows from investors and promises fixed cash flows.
Key Components
Face Value (FV) — amount repaid at maturity (usually $1,000)

Coupon Rate — annual interest as % of FV

Maturity — when FV is repaid (1yr to 30yr+)

Price — what investors pay today (≠ FV)
Cash Flow Timeline
t=0
−Price
t=1,2…
+C/2
t=n
+C/2+FV
Semi-annual coupons are the US standard.
C = coupon rate × FV (per year)
Price vs. YTM
Price = FV → Par · YTM = Coupon
Price > FV → Premium · YTM < Coupon
Price < FV → Discount · YTM > Coupon

Section 1 — Yield to Maturity
Price ↔ Yield: The Inverse Relationship
YTM is the single discount rate that makes the PV of all cash flows equal to the current price.
Why the Inverse Relationship?
If yields ↑ (new bonds pay more), old bonds less attractive → price ↓

If yields ↓ (new bonds pay less), old bonds more valuable → price ↑
Key Insight
YTM is like an IRR for a bond — it accounts for coupon payments and the difference between purchase price and face value at maturity.

In Excel: =RATE(n×2, C/2, −Price, FV) × 2
Section 1 — Example 1
YTM Calculation: Premium Bond
Bond trades above par — what does that tell us about yields?
Given Data
Price: $1,200
Face Value: $1,000
Coupon Rate: 15% / yr (semi-annual)
Maturity: 2 years
Periods: n = 4
Excel / Calculator
Semi-annual coupon = 15% × $1,000 / 2 = $75

=RATE(4, 75, −1200, 1000) × 2

= 4.34% per year
Interpretation
Price ($1,200) > FV ($1,000) → Trading at a premium → YTM (4.34%) < Coupon Rate (15%)
The high coupon relative to market rates makes this bond attractive → investors bid up the price.
Section 1 — Example 2
YTM Calculation: Deep Premium Bond
Same coupon, longer maturity — how does time affect YTM?
Given Data
Price: $1,500
Face Value: $1,000
Coupon Rate: 15% / yr (semi-annual)
Maturity: 10 years
Periods: n = 20
Excel / Calculator
Semi-annual coupon = $75

=RATE(20, 75, −1500, 1000) × 2

= 7.66% per year
Compare to Example 1
Longer maturity → higher YTM (7.66% vs 4.34%) — you must earn more to lock up capital for 10 years.
Price ($1,500) > FV → still a premium bond. YTM (7.66%) still < Coupon Rate (15%).

Section 2 — Benchmark Rate
T-Bonds: The Risk-Free Benchmark
Every yield in finance is quoted as a spread over the Treasury rate.
Why "Risk-Free"?
US Treasury bonds are backed by the full faith and credit of the US government.

The US has never defaulted on its debt.

Investors treat T-bond yields as the minimum return they will accept for any investment.
The Fundamental Equation
Any borrower with credit risk must pay
more than the risk-free rate.
The Credit Spread
The extra amount above the risk-free rate is the credit spread — it compensates investors for the possibility that the borrower might not repay. The higher the default risk, the wider the spread.
Section 3 — Credit Risk
Credit Spreads: Pricing Default Risk
Investors demand extra return for the possibility of non-payment.
The Basic Relationship
The spread compensates for:
• Probability of default (PD)
• Loss given default (LGD)
• Illiquidity premium
Mortgage Rates — A Real Example
30yr mortgage ≈ 10yr T-bond + ~250–300bp

When T-bond rate rises 50bp → mortgage rate also rises ~50bp → Higher monthly payments for homebuyers.

Section 3 — Case Study
Evergrande: When Spreads Warned First
China's largest property developer defaulted in 2021 — bond markets saw it coming.
The Collapse
Evergrande Group — Chinese real estate giant with over $300B in liabilities.

By mid-2021, bonds traded at 20–30 cents on the dollar, implying spreads of 2,000+ bp.

December 2021: Official default declared. Creditors recovered little.
Timeline of Distress
2019: Bonds near par, spread ~500bp — concern rising
2020: Spread widens to 1,000bp — market alarmed
Early 2021: Spreads blow out to 2,000bp
Dec 2021: Official default — market had priced it months earlier
Key Lesson
Bond markets are forward-looking. Credit spreads started signaling distress long before the official default announcement. Spreads are real-time risk gauges, not lagging indicators.

Why Ratings Matter
Many institutional investors (pension funds, insurance) are mandated to hold only investment-grade bonds (BBB− or above). A downgrade below BBB− forces forced selling.
Limitation
Rating agencies lag the market. Evergrande's spread blew out to 2,000bp while its rating was still investment-grade. Real-time credit spreads are the better signal.

Step 3: Credit Spread = Apple YTM − Treasury YTM → expressed in basis points (1% = 100 bp)
Section 4 — Exercise 2 · 3 Points
Apple Bond — Your Answers
Calculate Apple's yield to maturity and credit spread using the Excel RATE function.
Q1 (1 pt) — Apple Bond YTM (%)
=RATE(60, 2.225, −93.779, 100) × 2 → ≈ 4.84%
Q2 (1 pt) — Treasury YTM (%)
=RATE(40, 2.25, −105.25, 100) × 2 → ≈ 4.10%
Q3 (1 pt) — Credit Spread (%)
Apple YTM − Treasury YTM = 4.84% − 4.10% = 0.74% ≈ 74 bp
At ~74–84 bp over Treasuries, markets treat Apple (AA+) as nearly default-proof.
What We Learned — Fixed Income
What We Learned Today
1
YTM is the key metric. It's the single discount rate that prices all future cash flows. When price rises, YTM falls — the inverse relationship is fundamental.
2
The Fed sets the baseline. Every rate in the economy is anchored to the federal funds rate. When the Fed moves, mortgages, corporate borrowing costs, and bond prices all adjust.
3
T-bonds are the risk-free benchmark. Every rate in finance is anchored to the Treasury yield. Corporates and sovereigns pay a spread above it — the higher the credit risk, the wider the spread.
4
Credit spreads price default risk. i = rf + credit spread. Evergrande's spreads blew out to 2,000bp before the actual default — bond markets are forward-looking.
5
Ratings are a guide, not a guarantee. Agencies provide a useful baseline but lag the market. Real-time credit spreads are the best live signal of investor sentiment.
6
Apple trades near risk-free. At only ~84bp over Treasuries, markets treat Apple (AA+) as nearly default-proof — a perfect illustration of how credit spreads reveal perceived issuer quality.
