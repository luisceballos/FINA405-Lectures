Section 1 — Foundation
The Importer's Problem
A U.S. firm imports machinery from Germany — and worries about EUR/USD volatility.
😰 If EUR Appreciates vs USD
The firm owes €1,000,000 in 6 months.

Today: EUR/USD = 1.08 → cost = $1,080,000

In 6M if EUR = 1.15: cost = $1,150,000
Unexpected loss: +$70,000 vs. plan
😊 If EUR Depreciates vs USD
The firm owes €1,000,000 in 6 months.

Today: EUR/USD = 1.08 → cost = $1,080,000

In 6M if EUR = 1.02: cost = $1,020,000
Windfall saving: −$60,000 vs. plan
💡 The Solution: Lock in the Forward Rate Today
The firm can buy EUR forward — agreeing today on the rate it will pay in 6 months.
The fair forward rate is determined by Interest Rate Parity (IRP) — not by guessing.
Section 1 — Recap
Converting Foreign Cash Flows
Two approaches — one certain, one uncertain.
Method 1 — Wait for Spot Rate
Convert foreign CFs at whatever the spot rate is at maturity.

✗ You don't know what the rate will be.
✗ Exposed to FX risk — budgeting is impossible.
Method 2 — Use the Forward Rate
Lock in the forward rate today for delivery at maturity.

✓ Eliminates FX uncertainty.
✓ Enables precise budgeting and hedging.
Key takeaway: The forward rate is not a guess — it is derived from IRP, which we cover next.

Section 1 — Core Formula
Interest Rate Parity
The no-arbitrage relationship between spot rates, forward rates, and interest rate differentials.
F
Forward exchange rate
(quote-currency units per base)
S
Spot exchange rate
(today's rate)
T
Maturity in years
3M = 0.25, 12M = 1.0
Intuition: The forward rate compensates for the interest rate advantage of the higher-yielding currency. If iquote > ibase, then F > S — the quote currency trades at a forward premium.

Section 2 — Application
Multinational Case: TechGlobal Inc.
A U.S. tech firm must pay a German supplier €2,500,000 in 18 months and wants to hedge.
Given Data
EUR/USD Spot (S)1.0850
r_USD (domestic)5.25% p.a.
r_EUR (foreign)3.75% p.a.
Maturity (T)18M = 1.5 yrs
Step-by-Step Solution
F(18M) = 1.0850 × 1.07875 / 1.05625
F(18M) = 1.1081

USD cost of hedge:
€2,500,000 × 1.1081 = $2,770,250
Managerial takeaway: By locking in the IRP-implied forward rate today, TechGlobal converts FX risk into a known USD obligation — enabling precise financial planning regardless of where spot ends up in 18 months.
Section 2 — Hedge Mechanics
What Happens at Maturity?
TechGlobal locked in F(18M) = 1.1081. Spot at maturity determines the outcome — but the cost was always known.
😰 Scenario A — EUR Strengthened
Spot(18M) = 1.1500 > Forward 1.1081
Without hedge: €2.5M × 1.1500 = $2,875,000
With hedge:    €2.5M × 1.1081 = $2,770,250
Hedge saved: $104,750
✓ Hedge outperformed spot
😊 Scenario B — EUR Weakened
Spot(18M) = 1.0600 < Forward 1.1081
Without hedge: €2.5M × 1.0600 = $2,650,000
With hedge:    €2.5M × 1.1081 = $2,770,250
Hedge cost extra: $120,250
Spot was cheaper — price was fixed
Key insight: A forward hedge is insurance, not speculation. You give up upside to eliminate downside — and always know your cost in advance. A forward contract has zero upfront cost — no premium is paid at inception, unlike options.

Section 2 — When IRP Is Violated
Covered Interest Arbitrage
AUD/USD example — the market forward is mispriced. How large is the arbitrage?
IRP Calculator
Enter values above to see result.
The Arbitrage Mechanism
If F_market < F_IRP (forward is too cheap):
① Borrow foreign currency (e.g., AUD)
② Convert to domestic (USD) at spot
③ Lend domestic at r_domestic
④ Buy foreign forward at F_market
→ Earn risk-free profit at maturity

Self-correcting Arbitrageurs drive F_market → F_IRP until no profit remains. In liquid FX markets this takes seconds.

What Did We Find?
Three questions to bring it all together.
① Shape of the curve: Why does the forward rate diverge from spot as maturity increases? Which pair shows the largest deviation — and why?
② Market efficiency: If you found a market forward that differed from your IRP-implied rate by 0.005, could you profit? What would happen next?
③ Covered Interest Parity Puzzle: After the 2008 financial crisis, the EUR/USD basis turned persistently negative — meaning F_market < F_IRP, even in large liquid markets. Why didn't arbitrageurs eliminate this?
Summary
What We Learned
01
Rates drive FX: Higher domestic rates attract capital inflows → domestic currency appreciates.
02
IRP formula: F = S × (1 + iquote·T) / (1 + ibase·T) gives the no-arbitrage forward rate.
03
Covered arb: If the market forward deviates from F_IRP, a risk-free profit exists — until arbitrageurs close the gap.
04
Forward curves: IRP implies a full term structure — higher rate differentials create steeper forward curves.
05
Multinational use: Firms use IRP-implied forwards to eliminate FX uncertainty on future payables and receivables.
06
Real-world limits: Post-GFC, the covered interest parity puzzle showed that market frictions can sustain temporary IRP deviations.
